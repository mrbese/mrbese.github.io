*Data centers are the first electricity buyer that can move to the resource without giving anything up. That breaks the constraint that stranded American geothermal for forty years, and it was never the geology.*

Until now, geothermal lived and died by one number: the length of the cord between the wellhead and the wire. Fields near transmission got built. Fields that were not never got a look, whatever their steam was worth per kilowatt-hour. This piece is about the AI data center holding the wire cutters.

Geothermal fascinated me with 24/7 production, closer than the sun, literally right under our footsteps. A personal love affair, come full circle. And I was lucky. My professor in geothermal energy, Fusun Tut Haklidir, came from the corporate world, so the class was never just engineering. It was feasibility, profits, the corporate reality, and it hooked the entrepreneur in me at first sight. That is where I first learned the villain was never the geology. It was the geography.

That is why I wrote my thesis on geothermal energy, under Fusun's advisorship. Old habit of mine: of course I had to go to the source. The Stanford Geothermal Workshop, the field's flagship gathering, nothing to present, just to listen. And it is also why I refused to model my hometown field at Seferihisar on assumed numbers. The real production data sat privately held inside Ormat Technologies. Even the local government did not have it. I tracked down Gad Shoshan, Ormat's director for Turkey, and asked him for it in person. He shared it, and Ormat permitted its first publication in my thesis.

A master's at Columbia, energy audits in Los Angeles, then tech, then companies of my own. Geothermal stayed the first love. Today the circle closes, because the most modern load humanity has ever built is resurrecting the oldest firm power source we know. Not through a subsidy and not through a miracle well design. The buyer learned to move.

## when geography kept beating geology

*"A proven field sat idle for fifteen more years."*

Geothermal developers carry a rule of thumb that DOE wrote down in the GeoVision barriers report: one mile of gen-tie line per megawatt of plant. A 25 MW field justifies roughly 25 miles of wire. Past that, it usually strands, no matter how good the resource is. That ratio is a screen, the kind a gravel plant runs rock through: it sorts by size, not by what is inside, and steam was never a size. The Western Governors' Association graded about 140 prospects across the West in 2006; most of the near-market entries remain unbuilt, and the report's reason, "minimal existing transmission that can be used," is the polite way of saying nobody will ever pay for the wire. The rare field that beat the screen did it with brute force: Dixie Valley exists because two ventures merged into one 67 MW plant just to justify a dedicated 220-mile line.

McGee Mountain, Nevada, is the field that never beat it. Drilled wells, geothermometry above 150C, a GeothermEx assessment of 25 to 52 megawatts sustained for thirty years. A DOE-funded report closed the case in 2014 with one sentence: transmission is "the biggest problem hindering development." The nearest line was a single 115 kV rural co-op circuit, and interconnection penciled out at $180,000 to $485,000 per megawatt. A proven field sat idle for fifteen more years. Not for lack of heat. For lack of a wire.

Then, in September 2025 and again in March 2026, Ormat pulled new development permits for McGee Mountain. The geology did not change. The market around it did: data-center load is pulling clean-firm procurement across Nevada. The same company whose well data started my career is going back for the fields the wires abandoned. That is a market signal you can read from orbit.

## the wait was the new wire

*"The deepest pockets in the economy just became the most impatient buyer in it."*

Over 2,060 gigawatts sat in US interconnection queues at the end of 2025, and historically only about 13 percent of queued capacity ever reaches operation. LBNL's cost data explains why: withdrawn projects in PJM were facing $599 per kilowatt in interconnection costs against the $84 that completed projects paid, and in the non-ISO territories where American geothermal actually lives, $671. What kills projects is discovering, years into a queue, that you owe six hundred dollars a kilowatt you did not model. Rhodium Group counted 1.7 gigawatts of geothermal, about 40 percent of everything this country has ever installed, stuck in those queues right now, and modeled geothermal meeting 100 percent of projected US data-center demand growth at a 31 to 45 percent lower levelized cost, if the load sites on the resource.

Which is the new fact of the 2020s: the load can. Industry has tried this move once before. Aluminum smelters chased stranded hydropower a century ago, and it worked, but the constraint did not disappear. It just moved from the wire to the railroad that had to carry the metal out, so geography kept its veto. A data center ships its product over glass fiber at the speed of light. It is the first hundred-megawatt buyer that can move to the wellhead, serve itself behind the meter, and cut the cord that stranded everyone before it.

And the hyperscaler's real currency is not the electricity bill. It is time to compute. A buyer whose constraint is speed does not wait three years for a queue position, and it does not drill wells one optimized hole at a time either. It puts more rigs on the field in parallel, eats the learning curve across the fleet, and increasingly runs its own AI down the drill string. That technology deserves its own article. The point here is simpler: the deepest pockets in the economy just became the most impatient buyer in it, and impatience favors the resource you can start drilling tomorrow.

## cut the cords

*"If your load can flex for a utility, it can flex for its own power plant."*

The objection writes itself: data centers worship uptime, geothermal plants take outages. Both true. Now watch what the data centers actually signed. Duke's Nicholas Institute found roughly 76 gigawatts of new large load could integrate into the existing grid if operators accept about 0.25 percent annual curtailment, and Google has already signed demand-response agreements with Indiana Michigan Power and in the TVA region. If your load can flex for a utility, it can flex for its own power plant.

Let me say the quiet part before anyone else does: nobody has run a wireless hyperscale campus, and every signed deal so far keeps a cord to the grid. What the deals prove is the direction. Each contract moves more of the reliability burden from the wire to the site, and the engineering is island-grid discipline, solved on real islands every day, just not free. Batteries cover trips and transients. Spare capacity covers overhauls, because riding a two-week outage at 100 MW on batteries alone means 33,600 megawatt-hours of storage, an absurd bill next to one more 25 MW unit. Many modern plants, Ormat's and Fervo's Cape Station among them, are already built as several modular units so maintenance rotates while the rest keeps running. The crossover to fully islanded is an equation, not a slogan: avoided network upgrades and queue-years on one side, spare units, storage, fiber, and remote construction on the other. The queue keeps adding years to the first side of the ledger.

The proof of appetite already has one buyer's name all over it. Fervo's Project Red has served Google since 2023, its follow-on 115 MW Corsac Station was approved in 2025, Cape Station targets 500 MW in 2026, and Fervo's S-1 discloses a 3-gigawatt framework agreement with Google. Google signed 150 MW with Ormat through NV Energy in February. Meta matched the direction with 150 MW from Sage Geosystems and 150 from XGS Energy. Google's 24/7 carbon-free pledge closes the gas-turbine shortcut its competitors use to island quickly, and nuclear arrives on decade timelines while Project Red went from signed deal to serving electrons in about two years. Geothermal is the only clean firm power you can drill for.

## now drill, baby, drill

*"Remote basins are not empty of resource. They are empty of accidents."*

The known geothermal map inherits the transmission bias. USGS counted about 9 gigawatts of identified US hydrothermal resource in 2008. Its 2025 Great Basin update raised the ceiling to 135 gigawatts including EGS, up to 10 percent of US power capacity, mostly unproven, because most Great Basin systems have no surface expression. Roughly half of all utility-scale geothermal discoveries were accidents: agricultural water wells, mining drill holes. Two-thirds of the region's Quaternary faults have no well within a kilometer.

Remote basins are not empty of resource. They are empty of accidents. Proving a well costs millions, and under one-mile-per-megawatt a perfect remote resource was unsellable, so nobody spent the millions to look. A buyer at the wellhead flips that expected value positive for the first time in the industry's history, and Fervo's 3-gigawatt Google framework already sketches how the chicken-and-egg gets financed. The middle of nowhere used to be geothermal's curse. Now it is the one place a data center can start building without waiting in anyone's line. Finally, geology beats geography.

Ten years ago I had to charm a regional director to see a single field's data. Ten years from now, the fields worth seeing will have their buyers camped on top of them. Geothermal is about to get the shine it has deserved for forty years, and not because anyone finally felt sorry for it. The load simply cut the cord itself.

> The map was never a map of the resource. It was a map of the wires.
